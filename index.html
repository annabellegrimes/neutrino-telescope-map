Neutrino telescopes of the world

Where the world’s neutrino telescopes are, and which part of the sky each one sees best. Hover a card to fly the globe to a telescope, then explore how each one’s view of the sky changes with the neutrinos’ energy.

A planetary network

Neutrino astronomy is only just beginning: IceCube’s first cosmic neutrinos in 2013, and the first sources traced since, have opened a new way to look at the most violent places in the universe. Seeing them well takes more than one telescope. Each sees best in its own band of directions near its horizon, so telescopes spread around the globe watch different parts of the sky, and together they cover nearly all of it.

Combined into a single, planet-wide instrument, the high-energy Planetary Neutrino Monitoring System (PLEnUM), these telescopes would cover almost the entire sky, and the chance of detecting a source could improve by up to a hundred times over what IceCube achieves alone. The rest of this page shows why: which part of the sky each telescope sees, and how it changes with the neutrinos’ energy.

Source: L. Schumacher et al., “PLEνM: A global and distributed monitoring system of high-energy astrophysical neutrinos”, Proc. 37th International Cosmic Ray Conference, PoS(ICRC2021)1185, arXiv:2107.13534.

Why one telescope cannot see the whole sky

A neutrino telescope does not see equally well in every direction. It sees best in a band of directions around its horizon, set by two filters: the matter above it, which screens out a confusing background, and the Earth below it, which can stop the neutrinos themselves. How much each filter lets through depends on the neutrinos’ energy, so the band moves as the energy changes. Slide the energy, and drag the globe to see the band from any side.

To see where each telescope looks, switch the figure to Earth and sky or Sky from inside: each band lands on the sky as a ring. As the Earth turns, the rings sweep round the sky, and sources drift through them.

The upper edge

A shield of water against muons

  1. Muons outnumber neutrinos a million to one. Cosmic rays hitting the atmosphere make them, and their tracks look just like a neutrino’s.

  2. Water slows them down. A muon loses energy as it goes, a little to knocking electrons off atoms and, at high energies, much more to bursts of radiation:

    −dE/dX = a + b Ea ≈ 0.27 GeV per metre of water, b ≈ 3.5 × 10⁻⁴ per metre

  3. So it only gets so far. Adding that up gives a muon’s range:

    R(E) = (1/b) ln(1 + b E / a)about 2.4 km of water at 1 TeV, 7.5 km at 10 TeV

  4. The shield is thinnest straight up. At depth h (2.6 km for P-ONE), looking at an angle θ from straight up, there is this much water overhead:

    X(θ) = h / cos θ2.6 km straight up, 5.2 km at 60°, 15 km at 80°

  5. Higher energy, higher edge. The few muons that get through still drown out neutrinos from above, unless the neutrinos are energetic enough to stand out: muons thin out faster with energy (about E−3.7) than cosmic neutrinos (about E−2.5).

The lower edge

A wall of rock that stops neutrinos

  1. Only neutrinos cross the Earth, so tracks coming up from below are clean.

  2. But energetic neutrinos get stopped. The chance of hitting something grows with energy. The fraction that gets through a column of matter X:

    P = e−X / λ,   λ = 1 / (σ(E) NA ρ)λ: interaction length; σ: the neutrino’s size as a target, growing about as E0.36 above 10 TeV; NA: Avogadro’s number; ρ: density

  3. The deeper below the horizon, the more Earth. At an angle δ below it, a neutrino crosses a chord of the Earth (radius R⊕ = 6,371 km):

    s = 2 R⊕ sin δa few hundred km just below the horizon, 12,742 km straight through

On this page: a uniform Earth, with the interaction length L (in Earth radii) set by two round figures: straight through, fewer than 20% get across at 100 TeV and 5% at 1 PeV. So L = 2 / ln 5 ≈ 1.24 and 2 / ln 20 ≈ 0.67, joined by a power law:

L(E) = 1.24 × (E / 100 TeV)−0.27Now: L = Earth radii

The edge is where half the neutrinos still get through, e−s/L = ½:

δlow = arcsin(L ln 2 / 2)Now: δlow = below the horizon

Both edges are illustrations, not P-ONE’s measured sensitivity: the model leaves out the Earth’s dense core, neutrinos that scatter but carry on with less energy, and the telescope’s own efficiency.

How the figure is drawnWhat is to scale, the published band, and the turning Earth

  • ⤢Not to scale

    The cones and the horizon disk are drawn large, so they can be seen. The Earth is drawn small beside the sky.

  • ↕The band at one energy

    Its lower edge is where half the neutrinos still get through the Earth; its upper edge is a rough model of the muons. Both are illustrations, not P-ONE’s measured sensitivity. The equations are under “Why the band sits near the horizon”.

  • ≋The published band

    Where each telescope is best at discovering a source whose neutrinos span many energies (a spectrum falling as E−2). At its edges, the chance of a discovery halves. At about 1 PeV, the single-energy band matches it. After L. Schumacher et al.

  • Planned telescopes

    Dashed rings are telescopes planned or proposed. Their sites are approximate.

  • ◎The see-through sky

    The sky is so far away that the whole Earth is a point beside it. So each band’s directions are carried out from the Earth’s centre: the dashed rays.

  • ↻The turning Earth

    The Earth turns once round its axis (the dashed line through the poles) in a sidereal day, 23 h 56 m: the time the stars take to come back to the same place. IceCube sits on the axis, so its ring never moves.

  • ✦The two sources

    The blazar TXS 0506+056 was the first source of neutrinos found in the sky, by IceCube. The nearby galaxy NGC 1068 followed.

  • ∅Not shown

    Neutrinos made in our own atmosphere. They arrive from every direction and are the main background at low energies.
